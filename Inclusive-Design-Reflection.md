# Inclusive Design Reflection

## What I Learned

I learned that working with vulnerable populations requires more consideration than simply making a product functional. Focus Bear is designed to support people with ADHD, Autism, and others who may have difficulties with focus, motivation, organisation, or frustration tolerance.

I also learned that neurodivergent people can have different communication and working preferences. Clear instructions, predictable processes, and avoiding unnecessary information can make both the product and workplace easier to use.

## Working with Neurodivergent Users

When designing or communicating with neurodivergent users, I think it is important to keep information clear and easy to understand. Too much information at once can become overwhelming, so breaking tasks into smaller steps can make them easier to follow.

Predictable navigation, simple language, clear feedback, and avoiding unnecessary distractions can also reduce the amount of effort required to use a product.

## Communication with Teammates

I can also apply these ideas when working with teammates. I should avoid assuming that everyone communicates or works in exactly the same way.

For example, instead of giving a vague instruction, I can explain what needs to be done, provide the important context, and make the expected outcome clear. I should also be patient if someone needs clarification or prefers a different way of communicating.

## First-Person Experience

I reviewed a first-person account from a person discussing their experience with ADHD and productivity tools. One thing that stood out to me was that productivity systems can sometimes become another source of stress when there are too many tasks, reminders, or complicated steps.

This made me realise that a productivity tool should help reduce the user's mental workload rather than create more work for them.

## Possible Improvement for Focus Bear

One improvement I would consider is making reminders and tasks easier to manage when a user is already overwhelmed. Instead of presenting too many reminders at once, the application could prioritise the most important next action and keep the information simple.

This could help reduce cognitive load while still helping the user stay on track.

## Example of a Supportive Response

If a user was struggling to complete their routine, I would avoid blaming them or simply telling them to try harder. I would first acknowledge the difficulty and then help them break the task into a smaller step.

For example:

> "That's okay. If the full routine feels difficult right now, let's start with just the next small step. You don't need to complete everything at once. Once you've finished that step, you can move on to the next one."

I think this type of response is more supportive because it focuses on helping the person move forward rather than making them feel bad about struggling.

## Reflection

The main thing I learned is that accessibility is not only about technical features. The way information is presented and the way we communicate with people can also have a big impact.

In my work, I will try to keep communication clear, avoid unnecessary complexity, and consider how a task or feature might feel to someone who is already struggling with focus or executive functioning. I will also try to be patient and avoid making assumptions about how other people should work or communicate.
